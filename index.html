Selected work · Conair / Cuisinart

Six months, five problems, one very patient 3-D printer.

My co-op sat between design and test. I'd model a part in the morning, print it overnight, and find out at lunch the next day whether it survived contact with a potato. These five projects are the ones with the most to show: a consumer-safety redesign, a full-scale appearance mockup, a two-part silicone mold, an instrumented thermal study, and a competitive cook-quality benchmark.

01

Keeping fingers off a mandoline blade

A safety redesign of an existing mandoline slicer: iterating a hand guard and carriage in SolidWorks, printing each version, and testing it on real food until the blade stopped being the most interesting part of the tool.

Consumer safety SolidWorks FDM 3-D printing Design iteration Food testing

The problem

A mandoline is a fixed razor blade with a ramp pointed at your hand. The stock guard on the unit we started with was easy to skip, because it didn't hold the food well, so the fastest way to use the tool was the way that put fingertips closest to the blade.

The design goal was blunt: make the safe way also the easy way. If the guard grips the food better than a bare hand does, nobody takes it off.

What I did

  • Modeled a captive food holder and carriage in SolidWorks that rides on rails over the blade deck, so the food is driven forward without a hand ever crossing the cutting plane.
  • Printed and tested successive versions: open collars first, then a fully enclosed dome with a spring-loaded, spiked pressure plate to bite into the food and take up slack as it shortens.
  • Added a screw-mounted rail bracket so the carriage stays captured on the deck instead of lifting off mid-stroke.
  • Tested each revision on real produce, checking grip on the last inch of a potato, the part of the stroke where injuries actually happen.

02

A toaster oven you could hold before it existed

Translating a SolidWorks twin-cook oven model into a full-scale cardboard appearance mockup, accurate to a quarter inch, with a removable window fixture so the glass proportions could be swapped and argued about in person.

Rapid mockup SolidWorks Hand fabrication Design review

Why cardboard

A CAD render will tell you the oven is 16 inches wide. It will not tell you whether it fits under a cabinet, whether the door swing hits the backsplash, or whether the whole thing simply feels too big on a counter.

Cardboard answers those in an afternoon and costs nothing, so the expensive questions get settled before anyone commits to tooling.

How it was built

  • Pulled dimensions straight off the SolidWorks twin-cook model and flattened them into cut patterns.
  • Built the shell, cavity walls and both rack levels in double-wall board, holding ±¼ in against the CAD.
  • Made the front window aperture a removable fixture so different glass sizes and bezel widths could be dropped in and compared side by side.
  • Mocked the internal rack spacing so cookware clearance could be checked with real pans, not assumed.

The source model: the twin-cook oven assembly the mockup was measured from.

03

Designing the mold, not just the part

A two-plate injection mold designed from scratch in SolidWorks, 3-D printed, and used to shoot silicone parts, a compact way to learn parting lines, draft, alignment and gating on something small enough to iterate in a day.

Injection molding Mold design SolidWorks assemblies Silicone

The exercise

Designing a part and designing the thing that makes the part are different skills. A mold forces you to think in negatives: where does the parting line fall, which way does the material draft, how does the tool stay aligned under pressure, and how does the finished part come out without tearing.

A gummy bear was the ideal test subject: undercuts, compound curves, and no flat surfaces to hide behind.

What it took

  • Modeled the bear, then derived the cavity and core as a mated SolidWorks assembly so both halves stayed driven by the same geometry.
  • Placed the parting line along the bear's silhouette to keep the undercuts on the arms and ears releasable.
  • Added four corner alignment pins and matching bosses to keep the halves registered, plus side flanges to clamp against.
  • Printed both halves, shot silicone, and demolded, checking flash at the parting line and fill in the ears and feet, the last places to pack out.
04

Trusting the number before trusting the result

Calibrating eight thermocouple channels against a boiling reference, then using them to map hot spots inside a bare prototype and quantify what adding insulation actually bought.

Instrumentation Thermocouples DATAQ DI-808 Data analysis Thermal testing

Calibrate first

Eight probes reading eight different numbers tells you nothing until you know how much of that difference is the appliance and how much is the probes. So before any hot-spot mapping, all eight channels went into a common boiling reference and logged together at 2 Hz.

The run below is that check: ambient to plateau in about four minutes, held for five, then a slow cool as the bath gave up heat. The useful output isn't the plateau value. It's the spread between channels, and the systematic offset from the 212 °F reference.

What it showed

  • All eight channels agreed within 2.5 °F of one another across the plateau, tight enough to compare positions inside a cavity with confidence.
  • Each individual channel was stable to under 0.7 °F of noise, so a real thermal gradient wouldn't be lost in scatter.
  • Every channel read low against the 212 °F reference by roughly 3 °F, a systematic offset, correctable in post rather than a reason to distrust the rig.
  • One channel ("Top Lid") sat consistently highest and two sat lowest, so those positions got noted rather than treated as real hot and cold spots.

Then: mapping the cavity

With the probes trusted, they went into a bare-bones grill and air-fry prototype, foil-taped at fixed positions across the upper and lower cook surfaces so each run measured the same points, and the wire routing was kept identical between runs so it never became a variable.

And: what insulation bought

The prototype ran with an uninsulated housing, so heat left through the top as fast as the element put it in. I built a cardboard insulation shroud over the upper cavity, re-ran the same instrumented profile, and compared curves against the bare baseline to quantify the improvement in cook performance.

05

Letting the food settle the argument

Benchmarking a prototype grill against competitor appliances, and checking whether running its air-fry drawer at the same time cost the grill anything, by cooking the same food the same way under fixed conditions.

Competitive benchmarking Test protocol Design recommendations

The method

Cook-quality claims fall apart without controls. Every unit got the same cut of pork, the same thickness, the same starting temperature, the same time, and every result was photographed from the same angle under the same light, laid out in the same order.

Once the images are directly comparable, the differences are obvious to anyone in the room, and the conversation moves straight to why.

The prototype grills on a top platen and air-fries in a drawer underneath, so it can run both at once. To find out whether that shared load hurt the grill, one condition cooked chops on the grill alone and another cooked the same chops while a batch of tater tots ran in the drawer below.

What came out of it

  • Ran the prototype in two conditions, grill alone and grill with the air-fry drawer loaded, and benchmarked both against two competitor units.
  • Scored mark definition, edge-to-edge browning coverage, and uniformity across the four pieces in each batch.
  • Found the grill produced essentially the same sear whether or not the drawer was running underneath, so a user can cook both at once without giving anything up on the grill side.
  • Fed the findings back as design recommendations alongside the thermal data from project 04, so the visual result and the measured cavity behavior told the same story.

Four units, same protein, same protocol. The prototype's two rows are the grill alone and the grill with tater tots air-frying in the drawer below it, and the browning is effectively unchanged between them.
