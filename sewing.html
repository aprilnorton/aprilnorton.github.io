Sewing is where I learned to measure carefully, fit something to a real body or a real window, and accept that the first version is a muslin, not a failure. It is the same loop I run at work with CAD and a 3-D printer, just with fabric and a seam ripper instead of PLA and a deburring tool.

Commission

Curtains for my parents' house

My parents asked, which made this the first sewing project I had to get right the first time. Someone else's windows, someone else's house, no room to shrug and call it a lesson.

What it took

Curtains look like the easiest thing in the world until you are standing on a chair with a tape measure working out how much fabric a gathered heading actually eats. Every panel had to be measured against its own window, not against the one next to it.

The rest was pinning. A lot of precise, slow pinning, because a hem that drifts a quarter inch over six feet reads as crooked from across the room.

The details

  • Measured each opening individually and added allowance for the gather, the rod pocket and the hem.
  • Pinned and pressed every hem before sewing so nothing wandered under the needle.
  • Two different rooms, two different fabrics: an open weave natural linen for the kitchen pass through, a soft floral for the living room.
  • Hung and checked in place, because the only tolerance that matters is how it looks on the rod.

Self drafted

A lounge set reverse engineered from my own closet

Two pieces, two completely different approaches to getting a pattern: one traced off a garment I already owned, one printed and taped together from a real pattern for the first time.

Two ways to get a pattern

For the shorts I took a pair I already own and liked the fit of, traced around them, and added seam allowance. That is reverse engineering: start from the object that already works and recover the geometry that produced it.

The long sleeve top was my first time actually using a pattern. I printed it across a stack of pages and taped them into one full size sheet before cutting. Lining up tiled pages is finicky in exactly the way a printed part is finicky, and if the registration is off at the start, nothing downstream fits.

How I normally work

Most of the time I do not use a pattern at all. I cut, try it on, pinch where it is wrong, take it in, try it on again. Trial and error on a live fit test, which is slower on paper and much faster in practice.

  • Shorts: traced from an existing pair, seam allowance added by hand.
  • Top: tiled pattern printed, taped and cut, keeping the registration marks aligned.
  • Matched fabric across both pieces so they read as a set.
  • Fit adjusted on the body rather than on the pattern, which is still how I prefer to do it.

Solving a small annoyance

A jewelry pouch that stops necklaces tangling

A drawstring pouch with individual slots sewn into the lining, so every necklace gets its own channel and nothing comes out of a bag as one knot.

The problem

Necklaces thrown into any soft bag will find each other and tangle. The fix is not a better bag, it is stopping the chains from sharing a volume in the first place.

So the lining is divided into separate slots. Each chain sits in its own channel and the drawstring closes over the top of all of them.

The build

  • Cream floral outer with a pink gingham lining, because the inside should be as nice as the outside.
  • Individual sewn slots sized to hold one chain each.
  • Drawstring casing at the top so it cinches shut and travels flat.
  • Small enough to go in a bag without becoming a project to pack.

Ongoing

Most of what I make starts as something else.

A lot of my projects are refashions. I take clothes I already have or something I thrifted and cut it into a new piece, or take it apart and put it back together so it actually fits me. It is the cheapest possible way to iterate, and it means I am never starting from a blank page or a full price bolt of fabric.
